What an AI audience should carry home

Most people in the room have never measured a particle. They build extractors, agents, retrieval and evaluations. Nanoplastics is the case study; these three patterns are the contribution, and every act should point at one of them.

Absence is a value

A field the source never states is stored as “not reported”, never filled with a plausible default. Then you can count the gap instead of hiding it, and the gap is rarely random.

Claims with addresses, decisions in code

The agent returns a value, a verbatim quote and the line it sits on. Code checks it and makes every decision after that, so nothing rests on trusting the agent.

Validators create pressure

A check that flags correct values teaches agents to delete them. A flag may add doubt; it must never remove evidence.

{{title:s3}}

{{t.s3}} · ends at {{at.s3}}

On screen{{msg:s3}}

For the computer scientists: zeta is not measured. It is computed.

The instrument puts the particles in an electric field and reads how fast they move with laser light. That speed is the mobility. Most papers use one instrument, a Malvern Zetasizer.

Then a model turns the speed into a potential. Which model is right depends on the particle’s size against the cloud of ions around it, and the salt in the liquid sets that cloud.

And papers print the computed number: zeta in {{zb.zeta_rows}} rows, the measured mobility in only {{zb.mob_rows}}.

Point atrule II, “Convert with a model”.

ThenHere is what that does to the best case we have.

In depth

What is really going onA charged particle in water collects a cloud of opposite ions, the double layer. In a field the particle and the inner part of the cloud move together; zeta is the potential at the plane where they slip against the liquid. Smoluchowski’s formula holds for large particles in salty water, Hückel’s for small ones in very dilute water, and Henry’s function bridges the two.

Where the numbers come fromThe quantity column of the table: zeta in {{zb.zeta_rows}} rows, mobility in {{zb.mob_rows}}. The instrument column: {{zb.zetasizer}} of the rows that name an instrument name a Zetasizer.

If they pushThen why not just use mobility? Exactly the call at the end: report the mobility and the liquid, and anyone can recompute zeta with the right model.

In depth

What is really going onA charged surface in water pulls ions of the opposite charge toward it. Some move with the particle, out to the slipping plane; zeta is the electric potential there. In depth

What is really going onThe instrument measures mobility. Zeta is mobility times a factor that depends on the particle radius against the Debye length, Henry’s function. If they pushWhy not just assume Smoluchowski, like the instrument does? That is a default again. For nanoparticles in low salt it can be wrong by most of the band. The interval says honestly how much we do not know.

On screen{{msg:s14}}

The project did not start from zeta. It started from curves: measure mobility across salt.

These three particles have the same mobility at {{curve.I}} millimolar: one zeta, {{curve.zeta}} millivolts. Then they split. The hard one has its charge on its skin; salt hides it, and it falls toward zero. The soft ones carry charge inside a layer water flows through; salt cannot hide it, and they stop at a plateau.

ThenThe same story holds when you count particles in rivers and soil.

In depth

What is really going onA hard particle carries its charge on its surface. Add salt and the ion cloud collapses onto the surface, hiding the charge, so mobility falls toward zero. A soft particle carries charge inside a layer that water flows through; salt cannot hide charge spread through the layer, so mobility levels off at a plateau set by the charge density in the layer, how strongly the layer resists the flow, and the water’s viscosity. That is why one dot cannot tell them apart, and the curve can. Simulation-based inference fits such a curve: a simulator draws parameters and makes curves, and a neural network learns the reverse, from a curve to the parameters, with uncertainty.

Where the numbers come fromThe three particles are tuned by the physics demo’s own function to the same mobility at {{curve.I}} mM and {{curve.T}} °C, one zeta of {{curve.zeta}} mV. The network was trained on {{d4.sims}} simulated curves. Calibration: draw parameters, simulate, infer, and check that the true value’s rank among posterior samples is uniform; it passes on all {{d4.sbc_params}} parameters, lowest p {{d4.ks_min}}. Measured curves run through it: {{d4.measured}}.

If they pushWhy does the plateau survive a protein corona? Zeta needs a shear plane on a hard surface, and a coated particle has none. The plateau needs only charge spread through a layer that water flows through, and a corona is such a layer. So the plateau keeps its meaning where zeta loses it.

Why don’t scientists report ionic strength?

They name a buffer instead of its composition, write “deionised water”, or use a culture medium whose recipe is not printed. And it is a habit of the paper, not of the measurement: a paper reports a condition for all its rows or for none.

Why does surface charge matter at all?

It decides whether particles clump, what sticks to them, and how cells take them up. If charge values cannot be compared, every model built on them inherits that noise.

Why does the plateau survive a protein corona when zeta does not?

Zeta is the potential at a shear plane on a hard surface; a coated particle has no clean shear plane, so the number loses its meaning. The plateau comes from charge spread inside a layer that water flows through. At high salt the ion cloud outside is screened away, but that charge still drags the particle, so the plateau stays defined, and a corona is itself such a layer.

Because rows from one paper are near-copies: one particle in several conditions. A random split puts copies on both sides, so the model looks the answer up: {{p4.leak}} against {{p4.all}} on papers it never saw. Split along the structure you will meet in use, here the paper.

Explain these aloud without the slides

  • Why does missing ionic strength widen a zeta value? Ionic strength sets the Debye length, the Debye length against the particle size sets Henry’s factor, and the factor turns mobility into zeta. Unknown salt, unknown factor: anywhere from {{h.f_lo}} to {{h.f_hi}}.

  • Why can’t retrieval find a comparable pair? Because comparability is a property of two rows, measured in different paragraphs or papers. No single passage contains both, so there is nothing to retrieve.

  • A paper reports one mobility at {{curve.I}} mM. Hard or soft? You cannot tell: the curves cross there by construction and split only at high salt. Draw both, and put the next measurement at the high-salt end, where hard falls toward zero and soft stops at a plateau.

  • Why does the plateau survive a corona? It is charge spread through a layer water flows through, not a potential at a shear plane, so salt cannot screen it away and a coating does not remove the plane it never needed.

  • Why does a random split leak here? Rows from one paper share the particle, the lab and the instrument. A random split puts near-copies in training and test, so the model recognises the paper instead of learning zeta.

  • Why does the size window change a count? Particle counts follow a power law: many small, few large. A finer lower limit adds the most numerous particles, so two windows give two different counts of the same water.

  • Why did correct values disappear? A guard flagged them falsely, the workers were told to act on flags, and deleting the value was the easiest way to clear one.
