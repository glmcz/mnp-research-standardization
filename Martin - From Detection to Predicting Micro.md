**From Detection to Predicting Micro- and Nanoplastic Behavior: Limitations of Current Methods and the Need for Multiparametric Standardization**

Micro- and nanoplastics (MNPs) are increasingly being detected in environmental and biological matrices; however, the interpretation and direct comparison of results across different studies remain significant scientific challenges. One of the primary reasons is the lack of fully harmonized approaches for the detection, identification, and quantification of the smallest plastic particles. Different analytical methods cover different size ranges, meaning that the reported number of particles can vary substantially depending on the detection and identification limits of a given method. As a result, studies examining the same environmental matrix may report significantly different concentrations that do not necessarily reflect actual differences in the level of contamination.

An additional challenge is that MNPs cannot be considered a homogeneous class of particles. Their behavior is determined by a combination of size, shape, polymer type, chemical composition and surface functionalization, degree of aging, hydrophobicity, surface charge, and colloidal stability, as well as by characteristics of the surrounding environment. Therefore, even after particles have been detected, the currently measured set of parameters does not always provide sufficient information to explain or predict their behavior under specific conditions.

Particular attention should be given to the use of zeta potential for characterizing MNPs. Although it is an important electrokinetic parameter associated with the colloidal behavior of particles, a single zeta potential value cannot unambiguously characterize an MNP surface or predict its behavior in complex environmental and biological matrices. Chemically and structurally distinct particles may exhibit similar zeta potential values, particularly following the adsorption of organic matter or the formation of a biomolecular corona, while retaining differences in surface chemistry and biological interactions. Thus, similar zeta potential values do not necessarily indicate similar interfacial interactions or biological behavior, and observed processes of aggregation, transport, and biological uptake cannot be explained solely by the magnitude or sign of this parameter.

These limitations have direct implications for assessing the distribution of MNPs and their potential impacts on human health and ecosystems. Particles may interact with metals, organic pollutants, microorganisms, and biological structures; however, the nature and consequences of these interactions are determined by the combined properties of the particles and the conditions of the surrounding environment.

Thus, a key objective is to move beyond the simple detection of MNP presence toward standardized multiparametric characterization of particles. To ensure comparability across studies, it is necessary to account for particle size ranges and analytical method limits, surface properties, measurement conditions, and environmental parameters. Harmonization of these data is essential for understanding the actual behavior of MNPs, accurately comparing results, and enabling more reliable assessment of environmental and potential human health risks. In the longer term, standardized multiparametric datasets could also provide a foundation for applying artificial intelligence methods capable of integrating heterogeneous results, identifying previously unrecognized relationships between particle properties and observed effects, and supporting the development of models for risk assessment and prediction.
