02Act I · why standardise

A nanoplastic number compares only with four things stated.

The size range and the method’s limit, the surface, the measurement conditions, where and when. Each is missing somewhere today, and each moves the number.

I

Size range and method limit.

One river, two size windows: the small fraction outnumbers the large by {{w.ratio}}.

II

Surface.

One polystyrene label, two papers, every stated condition matched: {{c14.gap}} mV apart.

III

Measurement conditions.

For a zeta value, five: the liquid, its salt, its pH, the temperature, the instrument. All five for one value in {{five.complete_papers}} of {{pp.n}} papers.

14Act IV · nanoplastics: what the papers measure

Zeta is not measured directly: it is computed from a drift speed.

The instrument measures how fast a particle drifts in an electric field: its mobility. Zeta is calculated from that speed with a formula, and which formula is right depends on how much salt the liquid holds.

I

Measure the speed.

A field pulls the particle through the liquid; laser light reads its speed. Of the rows that name an instrument, {{zb.zetasizer}} name a Malvern Zetasizer.

II

Convert with a formula.

Smoluchowski’s, Hückel’s or Henry’s: which one fits depends on the particle’s size against the cloud of ions around it, and the salt sets that cloud.

III

Print one number.

Papers print the zeta in {{zb.zeta_rows}} rows and the measured mobility in {{zb.mob_rows}}: the computed number stays, the liquid it needs goes.

out/tables/d1_zeta_table.csv: quantity, instrument

Speaker notes

For the computer scientists in the room: zeta is not measured, it is computed. The instrument measures how fast the particle moves in a field, and a model from colloid chemistry turns that into a potential, a model that needs the salt in the liquid. Papers almost always print only the computed number.
